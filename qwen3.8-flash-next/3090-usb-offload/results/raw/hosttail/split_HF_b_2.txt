Elias had not spoken a word in three years. The lighthouse, perched on the jagged teeth of the northern coast, demanded silence as much as it demanded vigilance. Every night, he climbed the spiral stairs, his boots echoing against the cold stone, until he reached the lantern room. There, the great Fresnel lens waited, a crystal heart beating with light.

He polished the glass until his reflection stared back, distorted and lonely. Outside, the Atlantic churned, a black beast hungry for ships. Elias fed the flame, adjusting the wick with trembling, arthritic fingers. He knew every storm by its scent, every wave by its crash. The light was his only companion, sweeping across the dark water like a divine finger pointing the way home.

One night, a small fishing boat struggled against the gale. Elias watched through the binoculars, his heart pounding in sync with the rotating beam. He couldn’t save them, but he could guide them. He increased the intensity, pouring his own warmth into the flame. The boat turned, narrowly missing the rocks, and vanished into the safety of the harbor.

Elias smiled, a rare, cracked expression. He wasn’t just keeping the light; he was keeping hope alive, one rotation at a time.
