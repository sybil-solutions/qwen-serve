Elias had lived with the sea for forty years, his skin weathered like driftwood and his eyes the color of a stormy horizon. The lighthouse, a solitary sentinel on the jagged cliffs of Cape Vane, was his kingdom. Every evening, as the sun bled into the Atlantic, he climbed the spiral stairs, his joints creaking in harmony with the wind.

He polished the great lens until it gleamed like a diamond, then lit the flame. The beam swept across the dark waters, a rhythmic heartbeat cutting through the fog. Ships passed, their horns lowing in greeting, but Elias rarely saw faces. He knew them only by their lights, fleeting stars in his endless night.

One night, a violent gale battered the tower. The glass rattled, and the sea roared like a wounded beast. Elias stood firm, his hand steady on the railing, watching the beam pierce the chaos. A small fishing boat, tossed like a toy, struggled against the waves. Elias didn’t think; he acted. He signaled with the lamp, guiding the vessel toward the safer cove.

When dawn broke, the storm had passed. The boat was safe, its crew waving from the shore. Elias smiled, a rare, cracked expression. He wasn’t just a keeper of light; he was a keeper of lives. The sea had taken much, but it had also given him purpose. He descended the stairs, ready to sleep,
