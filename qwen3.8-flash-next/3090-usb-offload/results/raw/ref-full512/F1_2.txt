Elias had lived with the sea for forty years, his skin weathered like driftwood and his eyes the color of a stormy horizon. The lighthouse, a solitary sentinel on the jagged cliffs of Cape Vane, was his kingdom. Every evening, as the sun bled into the Atlantic, he climbed the spiral stairs, his boots echoing against the cold stone.

The light was not just a duty; it was a promise. A promise to the sailors navigating the treacherous shoals, a promise that someone was watching, that they were not alone in the vast, indifferent dark.

One night, a violent gale battered the tower. Rain lashed against the glass like shrapnel. Elias polished the lens, his hands steady despite the trembling earth. Through the howling wind, he heard a faint, desperate horn blast. He adjusted the beam, sweeping it across the churning waves until it caught the silhouette of a small fishing boat, listing dangerously.

He didn’t speak, for there was no one to hear him, but he whispered a prayer to the old gods of the deep. The beam held steady, a golden thread in the chaos. Hours later, the boat limped into the harbor, safe. Elias watched from the gallery, the storm finally breaking. He smiled, a rare, cracked expression, and began the long descent. The light would burn again tomorrow.
